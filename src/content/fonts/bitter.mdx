The **slab serif** was born shouting — Victorian advertising wood type, ink-hungry and proud of it. So there is something quietly subversive about **Bitter**, a slab designed not to shout at all, but to be read comfortably for three hundred pages on a device the size of a paperback.

**Sol Matas**, drawing for Huerta Tipográfica in 2011, aimed squarely at the exploding ebook market. Early e-ink readers did ugly things to delicate serifs; hairlines vanished, brackets clogged. Slabs were the obvious answer — sturdy, even-stroked, low contrast — but the existing ones carried too much nineteenth-century baggage: display proportions, cramped spacing, personalities tuned for posters rather than paragraphs.

Bitter kept the honest construction and discarded the noise. Its serifs are softly rounded rectangles that ease into the stems with the slightest bracket; its x-height is tall enough to stay legible at the sizes e-readers demanded; its rhythm is calibrated for *narrow* measures, because an e-reader column is nothing like a printed page. The result reads as contemporary rather than retro — an industrial voice speaking at conversational volume.

A decade later the family matured into Bitter Pro and then into this variable release, stretching from a 100 thin to a 900 black along one axis. That range matters more than it sounds: interface designers discovered that a well-behaved slab is superb for settings where sans-serifs feel bloodless — code documentation, data-heavy editorial products, design systems that want warmth without whimsy.

Bitter’s career is a small lesson in typographic evolution. Genres do not die when their original context disappears; they wait for someone to find them a new job. The slab that once bellowed from a circus poster now murmurs bedtime novels on a beach — and does it beautifully.
